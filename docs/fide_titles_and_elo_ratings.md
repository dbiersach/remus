# FIDE titles and typical Elo ratings

The Elo rating carries the name of Arpad Elo (1903 to 1992), a Hungarian-American
physics professor and chess master who designed it. Elo is his surname, not an
acronym, so it is written "Elo", not "ELO". US Chess adopted his system in 1960 and
FIDE in 1970. An Elo rating predicts results: two players with the same rating each
expect to score half the points between them, a player 200 points higher expects to
score about 76%, and a player 400 points higher about 91%. After each game, a rating
rises when the player scores more than that prediction and falls when the player
scores less, so beating a stronger opponent gains more points than beating a weaker
one. The scale has no maximum. The highest rating any person has held is 2882, set by
Magnus Carlsen in 2014, and the strongest chess engines rate above 3500 on engine
rating lists.

FIDE, the international chess federation, awards titles to players who reach a
minimum Elo rating. The class names below
the titles (Expert and Class A to Class D) come from national federations such as
US Chess, not from FIDE.

| Title | Minimum rating requirement | Typical rating range |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| **Super Grandmaster** (unofficial) | 2700+ | 2700 to 2850 |
| **Grandmaster (GM)** | 2500 | 2500 to 2650 |
| **International Master (IM)** | 2400 | 2400 to 2500 |
| **FIDE Master (FM)** | 2300 | 2300 to 2400 |
| **Candidate Master (CM)** | 2200 | 2200 to 2300 |
| Expert (not a FIDE title) | None | 2000 to 2199 |
| Class A player | None | 1800 to 1999 |
| Class B player | None | 1600 to 1799 |
| Class C player | None | 1400 to 1599 |
| Class D player | None | 1200 to 1399 |

## Ratings of everyday players

Most chess players never earn a title. The table below places everyday players on the
same Elo scale, from someone who has just learned the moves to a FIDE Master. The
ranges are estimates: club ratings differ from one federation to another, and many
casual players have no rating at all.

| Player category | Typical Elo range | Representative rating |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Casual player who knows the rules | 400 to 900 | 650 |
| Beginning chess club member | 600 to 1200 | 900 |
| Strong chess club player | 1200 to 1700 | 1450 |
| First-board player on a club team | 1500 to 2000 | 1750 |
| Average city chess club member | 1200 to 1600 | 1400 |
| Strong city chess club competitor | 1600 to 2000 | 1800 |
| City club champion | 1800 to 2200+ | 2000 |
| FIDE Candidate Master | 2200+ | 2250 |
| FIDE Master | 2300+ | 2350 |

## World chess champions

This table covers the open world championship in classical chess, starting with
Steinitz's recognized title in 1886. It is ordered by each player's first title win,
with separate reigns combined in one row. Women's, rapid, and blitz championships
are separate competitions. The title was vacant from Alekhine's death in 1946 until
Botvinnik won it in 1948.

From 1993 to 2006, there were two competing titles: the **Classical** championship
and the **FIDE** championship. Both branches appear below, with their overlapping
reigns labeled. Kramnik reunited the titles in 2006. Unless a branch is specified,
the dates refer to the undisputed title. See the
[world championship history and title lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Chess_Championship#World_champions).

The rating column gives a player's highest published **FIDE classical Elo** where
shown, rather than a live rating, rapid rating, or single-tournament performance.
For the first five champions, Botvinnik, and Smyslov, it gives a **Chessmetrics
historical estimate** of peak strength. These estimates reconstruct results from an earlier era; they are
not official FIDE Elo ratings or predictions of what those players would score
today. The two scales should not be treated as interchangeable. Historical values
are documented in this
[comparison of champions' rating peaks](https://lwsed.com/blog/greatest-world-chess-champion/);
recorded values can be checked in the linked player biographies and the
[peak FIDE rating list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_chess_players_by_peak_FIDE_rating).

Botvinnik and Smyslov reached their prime before official FIDE rating lists began.
Their entries therefore use historical estimates from their prime rather than
late-career FIDE ratings. A peak rating can occur before or after a player's reign.
The year in the rating column identifies the peak, not a title win. Current-title
status and career peaks are checked through November 15, 2026.

| Champion | Years as world champion | Highest Elo or historical estimate | Style, innovations, and contribution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Wilhelm Steinitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Steinitz) | 1886 to 1894 | About 2,826 (Chessmetrics; 1876) | Helped establish positional chess: build small advantages, protect weaknesses, and attack when the position supports it. His later play favored sound defense and patient preparation over attacks at any cost. |
| [Emanuel Lasker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanuel_Lasker) | 1894 to 1921 | About 2,878 (Chessmetrics; 1894) | A resourceful defender and practical competitor who adapted to different opponents and positions. His 27-year reign demonstrated the value of resilience, flexibility, and creating difficult decisions for an opponent. |
| [José Raúl Capablanca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Ra%C3%BAl_Capablanca) | 1921 to 1927 | About 2,877 (Chessmetrics; 1921) | Famous for clear positional judgment and exceptional endgames. He showed how coordinated pieces and small advantages could produce wins with apparently simple moves; his instructional writing made those principles accessible. |
| [Alexander Alekhine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Alekhine) | 1927 to 1935; 1937 to 1946 | About 2,860 (Chessmetrics; 1931) | Combined imaginative attacks with deep calculation and positional preparation. His opening innovations, including the defense bearing his name, and annotated games influenced generations of attacking players. |
| [Max Euwe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Euwe) | 1935 to 1937 | About 2,769 (Chessmetrics; 1936) | A logical, well-prepared player who could also handle sharp tactical battles. His books organized opening, middlegame, and planning ideas into clear lessons; he later served as FIDE president. |
| [Mikhail Botvinnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Botvinnik) | 1948 to 1957; 1958 to 1960; 1961 to 1963 | About 2,885 (Chessmetrics; 1945) | Made systematic opening preparation, analysis of his own games, and disciplined training central to professional chess. His strategic style and chess school shaped later champions; he also pioneered computer-chess research. |
| [Vasily Smyslov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasily_Smyslov) | 1957 to 1958 | About 2,800 (Chessmetrics; 1956) | Known for harmonious piece coordination, accurate calculation, and outstanding endgame technique. His games and endgame studies show how active pieces and carefully timed exchanges turn small advantages into wins. |
| [Mikhail Tal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Tal) | 1960 to 1961 | 2,705 (FIDE; 1980) | An imaginative attacker famous for sacrifices and complicated positions. He demonstrated the practical power of initiative: forcing an opponent to solve difficult problems can matter even when a sacrifice is not objectively best. |
| [Tigran Petrosian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigran_Petrosian) | 1963 to 1969 | 2,645 (FIDE; 1972) | Excelled at anticipating threats and preventing opponents' plans. His positional exchange sacrifices, giving up a rook for a bishop or knight to gain lasting control, expanded understanding of defense and piece value. |
| [Boris Spassky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Spassky) | 1969 to 1972 | 2,690 (FIDE; 1971) | A versatile player comfortable with direct attacks, quiet maneuvering, and endgames. His flexible approach showed how to choose a plan for the position rather than force every game into one preferred style. |
| [Bobby Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bobby_Fischer) | 1972 to 1975 | 2,785 (FIDE; 1972) | Combined exact calculation, deep opening preparation, and clear endgame technique. His 1972 victory brought worldwide attention to chess; he later introduced Fischer Random chess and promoted clocks that add time after each move. |
| [Anatoly Karpov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatoly_Karpov) | 1975 to 1985; 1993 to 1999 (FIDE branch) | 2,780 (FIDE; 1994) | A master of patient pressure, restricting opponents' pieces, and converting small advantages. His games demonstrate how preventing counterplay and improving a position gradually can be as decisive as a direct attack. |
| [Garry Kasparov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garry_Kasparov) | 1985 to 1993 (undisputed); 1993 to 2000 (Classical branch) | 2,851 (FIDE; 1999) | Combined energetic attacks, deep calculation, and extensive opening research. He helped make computer-assisted preparation central to elite chess, while his matches with Deep Blue brought human-versus-machine competition to a worldwide audience. |
| [Alexander Khalifman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Khalifman) | 1999 to 2000 (FIDE branch) | 2,702 (FIDE; 2001) | A versatile player with strong opening preparation who won the 1999 knockout championship. His detailed opening manuals and coaching helped players build coherent opening choices rather than memorize isolated moves. |
| [Vladimir Kramnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Kramnik) | 2000 to 2006 (Classical branch); 2006 to 2007 (undisputed) | 2,817 (FIDE; 2016) | A deep positional player and influential opening researcher. He revived the Berlin Defense in his 2000 victory over Kasparov, advanced the Catalan Opening, and reunited the world titles in 2006. |
| [Viswanathan Anand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viswanathan_Anand) | 2000 to 2002 (FIDE branch); 2007 to 2013 (undisputed) | 2,817 (FIDE; 2011) | Famous for rapid calculation, tactical clarity, and adaptability. His match preparation produced important opening ideas, and his success as India's first world champion inspired a major expansion of chess in India. |
| [Ruslan Ponomariov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruslan_Ponomariov) | 2002 to 2004 (FIDE branch) | 2,764 (FIDE; 2011) | A versatile player who combines [tactical calculation with positional judgment](https://www.chess.com/players/ruslan-ponomariov#playing-style). He won the FIDE knockout title at 18, showing composure and practical strength in short, high-pressure matches. |
| [Rustam Kasimdzhanov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rustam_Kasimdzhanov) | 2004 to 2005 (FIDE branch) | 2,715 (FIDE; 2015) | Combines careful preparation with concrete calculation and tactical resourcefulness. Beyond his knockout title, his opening analysis and work as a trainer helped Anand and later championship challengers prepare for elite competition. |
| [Veselin Topalov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Veselin_Topalov) | 2005 to 2006 (FIDE branch) | 2,816 (FIDE; 2015) | An ambitious attacker who seeks active pieces, complex positions, and winning chances with either color. His deeply prepared opening ideas and willingness to sacrifice material made his games influential examples of dynamic play. |
| [Magnus Carlsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnus_Carlsen) | 2013 to 2023 | 2,882 (FIDE; 2014) | Combines broad positional understanding with exceptional endgame skill and persistence. He repeatedly creates winning chances from apparently equal positions, showing the value of sustained practical pressure beyond opening preparation. |
| [Ding Liren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ding_Liren) | 2023 to 2024 | 2,816 (FIDE; 2018) | China's first open world champion combines [patient positional play with creative attacks](https://www.exeterchessclub.org.uk/content/lessons-ding-liren). His 100-game unbeaten run and championship victory illustrate both defensive resilience and the ability to take risks at critical moments. |
| [Gukesh Dommaraju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gukesh_Dommaraju) | 2024 to present | 2,794 (FIDE; 2024) | Became the youngest undisputed world champion at 18. Known for deep calculation, resourceful counterplay, and keeping difficult positions alive; his longer-term contribution to chess theory is still developing. |
